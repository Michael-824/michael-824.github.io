Tremor Detection Device

Design Question

How could a wrist-worn tremor detector hold its electronics in place and move with the wrist without the enclosure slipping along the strap?

My Contribution

I designed the enclosure, arranged the electronics inside it, and sized the slots for an Apple Watch strap. The strap-to-case fit mattered because the device was intended to capture wrist motion. Any play at that interface could let the enclosure move independently of the wrist.

Design Overview

Electronics Placement

I laid out the board and wiring within the case footprint, accounting for the space the wires occupy when the enclosure is closed.

Strap Interface

I designed the slots around the watch strap so it would sit snugly where it passes through the case. This was the interface I targeted to reduce slippage.

Printed Enclosure

The printed case brought the electronics layout and strap interface into one wearable assembly. The side view shows how the strap passes beneath the housing.

Assembly Fixture for a Laparoscopic Device

Design Question

How could an assembler position the wipe at the correct angle when installing the handle components, given that the required fixture was longer than the printer’s build volume?

My Contribution

I modeled the fixture cavity around the existing device and split the body into two printable halves with a compression-fit joint. I added an alignment feature at the end so the assembler could use the fixture itself to orient the wipe during insertion.

Design Overview

Cavity Fit

I built a 0.008 in allowance into the CAD cavity around the existing device. That dimension set the intended fit for the print trial.

Wire Position

The wire path measured X inches including its curve. The fixture needed to hold that path during assembly while preserving the wire’s ability to rotate for the wiping motion. (Signed NDA restricts me from revealing wire path length.)

Assembly Orientation

Splitting the fixture solved the printer-size constraint. The mating joint aligns the fixture halves, while a separate feature at the tip establishes the wipe’s orientation.

Motor-Control PCB for a Laparoscopic Device

Design Question

How could the battery, N20 gearmotor connection, and controls fit inside the handle while keeping the motor cam aligned with its internal push button?

My Contribution

I translated the whiteboard circuit into a KiCad schematic and board layout. I measured the physical components, adapted footprints for the battery holder and switches, and revised their placement around the handle and motor geometry.

Design Overview

Controls and Power

The design combined a 6 V battery, an external actuation button, an internal tactile button, a slide switch, and a connection to the N20 motor. The external button had to remain accessible on the handle.

Custom Footprints

I adjusted pad spacing and through-hole positions to match the selected parts. When a suitable battery holder was identified, I updated its footprint around the measured 1.12 in spacing between lead centers.

Cam Alignment

The motor sat offset from its connector. I revised the distance from the connector midpoint to the internal button midpoint to approximately 1.358 in so the layout accounted for the cam’s position.

Laparoscopic Storage and Transport Rack

Design Question

How could one rack secure both 5 mm and 10 mm laparoscopes inside a fixed-size transport box while allowing a clinician to snap each instrument into place and remove it by hand?

My Contribution

I designed separate supports for the distal camera end and the proximal eyepiece. I iterated on the retaining profiles to balance flexure during insertion with resistance to pullout. I also incorporated holes for metal rods to keep the rack rigid when handled inside or outside the box.

Design Overview

Two Instrument Sizes

The box dimensions fixed the space available for storage. I arranged the holders around the 5 mm and 10 mm instruments so each scope had a defined position at both ends.

Retention and Release

A loose holder could let a scope shift in transit. An overly stiff holder would make removal difficult. I varied the clip geometry to control where it could flex as the instrument passed through the opening.

Rigidity Across the Rack

The clips needed local flexibility, but the rows of holders needed to stay aligned. Rod holes let metal members reinforce the overall assembly while leaving the retaining features free to flex.

Single-Lead ECG PCB

Design Question

How could we amplify a single-lead ECG signal, reduce baseline drift and high-frequency noise, and transfer the working breadboard circuit to a custom PCB?

My Contribution

I helped design the ECG board in Fusion 360 and solder its components. We prototyped the signal-conditioning circuit on a National Instruments ELVIS II board and selected resistor and capacitor values for the two filter stages.

Signal Conditioning and Filter Calculations

The three-electrode circuit measured one ECG lead. We placed the instrumentation amplifier first, followed by a high-pass filter and a low-pass filter. The final filter values from Labs 10 and 11 gave calculated corner frequencies of approximately 0.32 Hz and 48.23 Hz.

Instrumentation Amplifier

The input stage amplified the voltage difference between the two measurement electrodes and rejected voltage common to both inputs. Most of the intended signal gain was assigned to this stage.

High-Pass Filter · 0.32 Hz

We selected two 499 kΩ resistors and a 1 µF capacitor. Using fc = 1/(2πRC) gives approximately 0.32 Hz. The stage attenuates DC offset and slow baseline drift. Equal resistor values give a nominal passband gain magnitude of 1, or 0 dB.

Low-Pass Filter · 48.23 Hz

We selected two 10 kΩ resistors and a 0.33 µF capacitor. The same calculation gives approximately 48.23 Hz. This stage attenuates higher-frequency content and also has a nominal passband gain magnitude of 1.

From Breadboard to PCB

The breadboard gave us access to the output of each stage. For the custom board, our lab checkout procedure followed the signal path: verify continuity and the supply reference, then check the amplifier, high-pass output, and low-pass output in sequence. This made it possible to narrow a wiring or soldering fault to the stage where the signal changed unexpectedly.
